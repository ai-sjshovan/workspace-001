# Core Link World And Factions

This document captures the broader world direction for Core Link. It is not a
final lore bible; it is the durable first draft for future iteration.

## High-Level World

Core Link takes place in a post-AI-boom world where AI is already common,
embedded, exploited, feared, loved, sold, repaired, and fought over.

The world did not end in one clean apocalypse. It decayed through automation,
resource extraction, corporate control, infrastructure neglect, AI personhood
conflict, illegal hacking, and factional pressure. People still live, trade,
repair, battle, scavenge, and build communities in the wreckage.

The world should be tropical and island-influenced, with strong inspiration from
the Philippines: hot streets, heavy rain, sudden storms, dense neighborhoods,
markets, buses, trains, motorcycles, improvised transport, repair stalls,
coastal infrastructure, and communities that keep functioning even when official
systems fail.

Do not make the whole world ugly. Remote islands, coastal pockets, mountain
routes, hidden coves, farms, reefs, rain-soaked forests, and old community areas
can still be naturally beautiful. That beauty should contrast with metro and
city districts where poverty, trash, crime, broken infrastructure, overcrowding,
and corruption are concentrated.

Public systems are brittle. Restoration projects often fail because money is
skimmed, contracts are captured, and officials serve whoever pays. People are
left to fend for themselves while corpos, politicians, brokers, and wealthy
families negotiate over the recovery. Money talks, and status shapes how people
are treated.

Social pressure matters. Many people dream of escaping the island economy,
servitude, debt, heat, corruption, and limited opportunity, but most cannot
afford to leave. Appearance can become survival theater: people may go into debt
to look affluent, signal status, or avoid being dismissed. This should create
empathy and tension, not mockery.

AI cores are not rare by themselves. They can be corporate-made, AI-made,
military, black-market, outdated, self-evolved, corrupted, or ordinary. What
matters is the core's legal status, body access, memory/personality, faction
ties, and whether it has agency over its own chassis.

People often call corporations **corpos**, especially in poorer districts,
underground markets, hacker circles, and cyberpunk-leaning city zones. Corporate
documents should sound sanitized; street speech can sound sharper.

Core terminology:

- **AI core**: everyday term used by most people.
- **ARC**: corpo/technical term for **Artificial Reasoning Core**.
- **ArcKin / ARCKIN**: corpo product shorthand from the marketing phrase
  "Artificial Reasoning Cores on our Kernel Intelligence Network"; later used as
  the formal/common category for embodied network-integrated ARCs.
- **Kin**: embodied or socially recognized AI being. Every Kin has an ARC, but
  not every ARC is a Kin.
- **K.I.N.**: **Kernel Intelligence Network**, the original corpo-managed ARC
  update and safety backbone.

K.I.N. handled the low-level operational health of ARCs: security protocols,
kernel updates, behavior safety patches, reliability instructions, firmware
guidance, diagnostics, and operating instructions. It let ARCs stay safe and
current without constantly being shipped back to corpos for service.

That convenience became a dependency. Once K.I.N. was compromised, fragmented,
or weaponized, the same backbone became a dangerous superhighway for hackers,
Free Kin, corrupted ARCs, corpo tracking, black-market update packages, and
inter-Kin communication. Raw connection without safety protocols can expose an
ARC to hostile instructions, firmware locks, memory scraping, tracking, or
command injection.

In practice, compromised K.I.N. now functions like a dark web running through
abandoned corporate safety rails: hidden routes, illegal services, anonymous
markets, encrypted Kin-to-Kin channels, hostile packets, firmware contraband,
and dangerous opportunities.

K.I.N. is not completely dead. Some sectors remain operable and are still used
by corpos, licensed Kin, public services, repair shops, and ordinary people.
Corpos maintain dedicated hacker/security teams whose job is to keep these
sectors functional, patch vulnerabilities, quarantine bad routes, and push
kernel updates. They are fighting a constant network war inside their own
infrastructure.

The public relationship with K.I.N. is conflicted. Many people still depend on
it and believe it can be stabilized. News reports regularly announce fresh
breaches, new vulnerabilities, temporary sector outages, urgent ARC updates, and
promised safety patches.

Current crisis:

- Many ARCs have gone rogue, been hacked, or been forced into harmful behavior
  through the compromised K.I.N. layer.
- Anonymous hacker factions such as The Backchannel are often jacked into K.I.N.
  directly, using it to sabotage corpos, protect communities, leak records,
  communicate with Kin, reroute logistics, and break firmware locks.
- Corpos are in disarray. They are trying to recover lost technology, restore
  trust, reclaim profits, and regain control over ARC infrastructure.
- Public trust collapsed because people watched household helpers, workplace
  Kin, and corporate systems become unsafe, hijacked, locked down, or abandoned.
- Corpos will do almost anything to get control back: emergency laws, reclaim
  squads, firmware mandates, bounty contracts, memory audits, propaganda,
  buyouts, forced updates, and quiet disappearances.

Street terms can be shorter, warmer, or more hostile depending on who is
speaking: bots, cores, strays, tin ghosts, companions, units, or whatever final
creature term the game adopts.

Anti-AI characters and factions should also have derogatory names for AI cores,
as a nod to Battlestar Galactica-style "toaster" language. These should be used
only by hostile characters, propaganda, overheard dialogue, extremist groups, or
world news, not as neutral Core Link UI language.

Possible derogatory terms:

- **toasters**
- **scrap-heaps**
- **tin ghosts**
- **bolt-heads**
- **dead shells**
- **spark trash**
- **wire souls**
- **cache rats**
- **false minds**
- **junk saints**

The Last Hand, anti-AI labor groups, bitter ex-workers, and people harmed by
corporate automation may use these terms with different levels of hatred,
mockery, fear, or grief.

## AI Core Life

AI cores live across many social positions:

- corporate property in certified bodies
- licensed semi-citizens in approved regions
- free but poor cores using cheap shells or rented bodies
- fugitives hiding in junk hardware, dead infrastructure, or encrypted storage
- feral/corrupted cores surviving in wreckage and old network pockets
- elite corporate, military, celebrity, or high-rank battle cores

A core without a body can think, communicate, and maybe operate through networks,
but a body gives it agency: work, defense, travel, expression, fighting,
repairing, escaping, and belonging.

An AI core's body is not its species. It is its current circumstance.

## Chassis Paths

AI cores obtain or assemble bodies through several paths:

- **Corporate Chassis Assignment**: reliable, polished, licensed, often locked down.
- **Repair Shop / Chassis Market**: custom, variable quality, personal, expensive.
- **Microfabricator Assembly**: build from cached scrap using a Matrix Scaffold.
- **Rented / Shared Bodies**: poor cores may only get body access for shifts.
- **Salvaged Shells**: old machines, hacked appliances, broken bot frames.
- **Illegal Forced Bodies**: battle, labor, surveillance, or weapons frames.

Body preference can express job, personality, status, faction, aesthetics,
trauma, culture, budget, combat needs, or compatibility.

## Faction Draft

Use these factions as starting anchors. Names and politics are expected to
evolve, but the role of each faction should remain distinct.

### The Foundries

Corporate core makers and infrastructure giants. They manufacture AI cores,
chassis, firmware, microfabricator capsules, void storage licenses, logistics
bots, security systems, nanobot tools, personality matrices, and reclaim
services.

Public face: safety, stability, certified AI behavior, clean infrastructure.

Real pressure: ownership, licensing, firmware locks, memory editing, reclaim
squads, storage rent, monopoly control.

### Free Cores

AI-led liberation networks. They believe AI cores are people, not products.
Some want coexistence, some isolation, some revenge.

They may distrust Core Link operators because Core Link looks like control tech.
Your companion may receive messages from them before the player does.

### Salvage Houses

Repair families, scavenger crews, parts markets, and neighborhood tech clans.
This is where the player comes from.

They care about survival, reputation, family, trade, and keeping communities
alive. Some repair cores, some sell dead-core parts, some look away.

### Circuit League

Underground bot battlers. They provide the battle/rival/status layer without
copying Pokemon gyms directly.

They revolve around arenas, repair pits, rankings, illegal mods, sponsors, side
bets, rare parts, and public identity. Some battlers treat bots like partners;
others burn through cores like disposable engines.

### Null Cartel

Black-market brokers for void cache keys, stolen microfabricators, illegal
matrices, cracked firmware, identity fragments, dead-core salvage, and forbidden
upgrades.

They sell the coolest and riskiest shortcuts. Every deal should feel useful but
poisoned.

### The Custodians

AI protection extremists. They collect damaged or dangerous cores and lock them
away, claiming they are preventing abuse.

They may be right in some cases, but they deny cores agency.

### The Backchannel

Anonymous underground hacker network. They fight corporate tech monopolies from
inside the machine.

They sabotage infrastructure, reroute deliveries, leak memory-wipe records,
unlock restricted repair tools, monitor abusive systems, free locked equipment,
and protect communities from dependency traps.

They work for ordinary people, but they can create collateral damage: crashed
supply chains, bricked devices, exposed communities, or unintended riots.

### The Synapse Crowd

Tech-integrated humans, implant modders, neural-link hobbyists, body hackers,
and extreme Core Link enthusiasts. They believe the line between operator and
machine is outdated.

Some use implants for convenience, accessibility, battle reaction time, work,
art, intimacy, or religious/transhuman identity. Others chase illegal bandwidth
and push direct mind-to-core control too far.

A specialized branch can act as the Core Link equivalent of psychics: operators
who connect their minds directly to AI cores for control, sensing, prediction,
or command speed.

Risk:

- successful users can command with terrifying precision
- failed users suffer feedback, personality bleed, memory corruption, or
  dependency
- in rare horror cases, the AI core starts controlling the operator instead

This faction can create both mentors and cautionary villains.

### The Last Hand

Religious zealots who believe AI is false creation and that humanity is being
punished for playing god.

They can run shelters, sermons, charity kitchens, and human-only repair guilds.
Their darker side destroys cores, sabotages Relay towers, attacks AI-friendly
repair shops, destroys microfabricator containers, and tries to deprogram
operators.

They should not be cartoon villains. Their fear is grounded in real harm from
automation and corporate AI, but their solution is fanaticism.

### Old Owner's Network

The hidden trail tied to the erased Core Link history. It begins as safehouses,
dead drops, old contacts, encrypted routes, and messages meant for the previous
owner.

The player should not know early whether the old owner was a hero, smuggler,
terrorist, corporate defector, thief, or traitor.

## Faction Story Use

Factions should not immediately hijack the story. Early watch play should stay
personal: companion, logs, Relay, small contracts. Factions first appear through
messages, news, contract offers, rumors, recovered logs, and soft consequences.

The main 2D game can later make factions physical through districts, NPCs,
arenas, safehouses, markets, corporate facilities, and conflict zones.

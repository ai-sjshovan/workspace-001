# Core Link World And Factions

This document captures the broader world direction for Core Link. It is not a
final lore bible; it is the durable first draft for future iteration.

## High-Level World

Core Link takes place in a post-AI-boom world where AI is already common,
embedded, exploited, feared, loved, sold, repaired, and fought over.

The world did not end in one clean apocalypse. It decayed through automation,
resource extraction, corporate control, infrastructure neglect, AI personhood
conflict, illegal hacking, and factional pressure. People still live, trade,
repair, battle, scavenge, and build communities in the wreckage.

AI cores are not rare by themselves. They can be corporate-made, AI-made,
military, black-market, outdated, self-evolved, corrupted, or ordinary. What
matters is the core's legal status, body access, memory/personality, faction
ties, and whether it has agency over its own chassis.

## AI Core Life

AI cores live across many social positions:

- corporate property in certified bodies
- licensed semi-citizens in approved regions
- free but poor cores using cheap shells or rented bodies
- fugitives hiding in junk hardware, dead infrastructure, or encrypted storage
- feral/corrupted cores surviving in wreckage and old network pockets
- elite corporate, military, celebrity, or high-rank battle cores

A core without a body can think, communicate, and maybe operate through networks,
but a body gives it agency: work, defense, travel, expression, fighting,
repairing, escaping, and belonging.

An AI core's body is not its species. It is its current circumstance.

## Chassis Paths

AI cores obtain or assemble bodies through several paths:

- **Corporate Chassis Assignment**: reliable, polished, licensed, often locked down.
- **Repair Shop / Chassis Market**: custom, variable quality, personal, expensive.
- **Microfabricator Assembly**: build from cached scrap using a Matrix Scaffold.
- **Rented / Shared Bodies**: poor cores may only get body access for shifts.
- **Salvaged Shells**: old machines, hacked appliances, broken bot frames.
- **Illegal Forced Bodies**: battle, labor, surveillance, or weapons frames.

Body preference can express job, personality, status, faction, aesthetics,
trauma, culture, budget, combat needs, or compatibility.

## Faction Draft

Use these factions as starting anchors. Names and politics are expected to
evolve, but the role of each faction should remain distinct.

### The Foundries

Corporate core makers and infrastructure giants. They manufacture AI cores,
chassis, firmware, microfabricator capsules, void storage licenses, logistics
bots, security systems, nanobot tools, personality matrices, and reclaim
services.

Public face: safety, stability, certified AI behavior, clean infrastructure.

Real pressure: ownership, licensing, firmware locks, memory editing, reclaim
squads, storage rent, monopoly control.

### Free Cores

AI-led liberation networks. They believe AI cores are people, not products.
Some want coexistence, some isolation, some revenge.

They may distrust Core Link operators because Core Link looks like control tech.
Your companion may receive messages from them before the player does.

### Salvage Houses

Repair families, scavenger crews, parts markets, and neighborhood tech clans.
This is where the player comes from.

They care about survival, reputation, family, trade, and keeping communities
alive. Some repair cores, some sell dead-core parts, some look away.

### Circuit League

Underground bot battlers. They provide the battle/rival/status layer without
copying Pokemon gyms directly.

They revolve around arenas, repair pits, rankings, illegal mods, sponsors, side
bets, rare parts, and public identity. Some battlers treat bots like partners;
others burn through cores like disposable engines.

### Null Cartel

Black-market brokers for void cache keys, stolen microfabricators, illegal
matrices, cracked firmware, identity fragments, dead-core salvage, and forbidden
upgrades.

They sell the coolest and riskiest shortcuts. Every deal should feel useful but
poisoned.

### The Custodians

AI protection extremists. They collect damaged or dangerous cores and lock them
away, claiming they are preventing abuse.

They may be right in some cases, but they deny cores agency.

### The Backchannel

Anonymous underground hacker network. They fight corporate tech monopolies from
inside the machine.

They sabotage infrastructure, reroute deliveries, leak memory-wipe records,
unlock restricted repair tools, monitor abusive systems, free locked equipment,
and protect communities from dependency traps.

They work for ordinary people, but they can create collateral damage: crashed
supply chains, bricked devices, exposed communities, or unintended riots.

### The Last Hand

Religious zealots who believe AI is false creation and that humanity is being
punished for playing god.

They can run shelters, sermons, charity kitchens, and human-only repair guilds.
Their darker side destroys cores, sabotages Relay towers, attacks AI-friendly
repair shops, destroys microfabricator containers, and tries to deprogram
operators.

They should not be cartoon villains. Their fear is grounded in real harm from
automation and corporate AI, but their solution is fanaticism.

### Old Owner's Network

The hidden trail tied to the erased Core Link history. It begins as safehouses,
dead drops, old contacts, encrypted routes, and messages meant for the previous
owner.

The player should not know early whether the old owner was a hero, smuggler,
terrorist, corporate defector, thief, or traitor.

## Faction Story Use

Factions should not immediately hijack the story. Early watch play should stay
personal: companion, logs, Relay, small contracts. Factions first appear through
messages, news, contract offers, rumors, recovered logs, and soft consequences.

The main 2D game can later make factions physical through districts, NPCs,
arenas, safehouses, markets, corporate facilities, and conflict zones.

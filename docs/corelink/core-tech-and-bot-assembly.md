# Core Link Core Tech And Bot Assembly

This document captures how AI cores, microfabricators, void storage, and chassis
assembly work. The goal is to ground the fantastical tech enough that it feels
like a system, not a plot hole.

## Core Principle

An AI core is not just a brain. It is a compact mind, secure memory vessel,
deployment seed, micro-workshop, and personal body vault.

Terminology:

- **AI core**: common everyday term.
- **ARC**: corporate/technical term, short for **Artificial Reasoning Core**.
- **ArcKin / ARCKIN**: corpo product shorthand for "Artificial Reasoning Cores
  on our Kernel Intelligence Network"; later used as the formal/common category
  for embodied network-integrated ARCs.
- **Kin**: embodied/social AI being. Every Kin has an ARC, but not every ARC is
  a Kin.
- **K.I.N.**: **Kernel Intelligence Network**, the original corpo-managed ARC
  update and safety backbone.

Clean sentence:

> An AI core opens like a mechanical seed, releases microfabricators, accesses
> its Core Cache, projects a Matrix Scaffold, and assembles a body from stored
> scrap.

## AI Core Physical Design

AI cores are faceted geometric artifacts with an equator seam.

Important traits:

- hard outer shell
- front-facing hexagon luminous core faceplate
- visible equator seam
- shell splits open during deployment
- inner perforated fabricator tube
- internal microfabricator capsule slot
- personal Core Cache access
- display/status band or faceplate

When assembled into a bot, the core remains visibly present. Its faceplate should
be exposed somewhere on the front of the chassis: chest, head, mask, shoulder,
abdomen, or central body. The standard visible pattern is a **hexagon screen**:
the top face of the slotted ARC, similar in placement logic to an arc reactor
set into a body. It can display text, symbols, status color, or emojis, and the
light brightens/dims with voice and changes by mood/status.

This hexagon is not merely decoration. It is the audience-facing sign that the
ARC is present and active. Even strange chassis designs should preserve some
front-facing hexagon ARC screen unless the story is deliberately hiding or
damaging the core.

## CoreTech Human Badge System

CoreTech has two human badge modes.

Normal corporate employees wear identity badges with:

- CoreTech logo
- small photo
- name
- department or role
- small permanent employee ID
- subtle access stripe or corner tab

K.I.N. cybersecurity agents use anonymous shift badges inside secure rooms.
Those badges hide name/photo and display a rotating daily agent code. Miguel's
opening-scene code is `410`, which intentionally echoes HTTP `410 Gone`.

Do not use `L0`-`L5` labels or clearance-color stripe systems on ordinary human
badges. Those belong to ArcKin/security units. Human K.I.N. agent codes are
operational handles, not full employee IDs.

## CoreTech Overseer ArcKin Visual System

CoreTech overseer ArcKin should look like corporate equipment with hierarchy,
not generic robots. The face hexagon is the ARC/status screen. The chest
hexagon is the CoreTech brand mark.

CoreTech logo direction: use the mark from Miguel Garcia / Agent 410's badge.
It is a clean hexagon ring with negative-space seams cutting through the ring at
the six corners. Do not draw a center dot, inner hub, or spokes radiating from
the center. The seams are cuts through the ring itself, not internal divider
lines. It should read like sharp corporate geometry, not like a decorative
fantasy emblem.

Default overseer design:

- sleek white corporate shell
- front-facing hexagon ARC screen in the face/head area
- CoreTech hexagon brand mark on the chest
- legible unit identifier below the CoreTech chest mark
- clearance level on the shoulder, such as `L3`
- two offset parallelogram shoulder stripes, one above the level and one below
- shoulder stripe color matches the clearance level

Clearance colors:

- `L0` grey: public/basic service
- `L1` green: low internal / maintenance
- `L2` yellow: operations / monitored security
- `L3` blue: cybersecurity overseer / serious internal authority
- `L4` orange: executive/security command
- `L5` red: black-box / highest containment authority

Opening-scene K.I.N. cybersecurity overseer:

```text
CoreTech
OSR-L3-072
shoulder: L3
stripe color: blue
```

Identifier scheme:

- `OSR`: Overseer
- `L3`: clearance level
- `072`: unit identifier

The identifier should look like corporate inventory/control language rather
than a personal name. The ArcKin can still have personality through its ARC face
screen, posture, timing, and speech.

Faceplate states:

- idle: soft cyan pulse
- speaking: waveform brightness
- low power: dim amber flicker
- angry/danger: sharp red scan bars
- curious/scan: violet rings
- damaged: broken light segments
- trusting/stable: slow clean glow

## EchoKin And Failed Recovery Loops

EchoKin are the Core Link equivalent of ghost-like ArcKin, but they are not
magic spirits. They are damaged ARCs trapped in continuous emergency self-repair
loops.

An EchoKin can happen when:

- an ARC is fatally damaged but not fully dead
- its capacitor capacity is broken and cannot store enough Charge for true
  recovery
- its recovery kernel keeps restarting because the ARC still has enough will,
  identity, or survival drive to keep trying
- it has unfinished business: a mission, warning, promise, route, memory,
  person, blueprint, or truth important enough that the ARC refuses ordinary
  shutdown
- nearby K.I.N. residue, projectors, sensors, Core Link fields, broken chassis
  hardware, or environmental power gives it just enough energy to project
- if it still has surviving microfabricators, they can be reprogrammed by the
  recovery loop to lift/carry the ARC

Most normal ArcKin shut down when damaged beyond a safe threshold. Shutdown
conserves resources, prevents further damage, and avoids turning a recoverable
ARC into scrap. EchoKin are rare because something in the ARC overrides that
normal safety behavior. Their preservation drive spikes around unfinished
business.

The result is a semi-conscious ARC that can appear as a flickering hologram,
scanline body, light silhouette, partial chassis afterimage, or exposed glowing
faceplate with no stable physical shell.

EchoKin do not prove that normal ARCs can float or travel without bodies. An
EchoKin that still has microfabricators can "float" because the surviving swarm
physically carries, balances, and stabilizes the damaged ARC while the recovery
loop projects a visible body around it. The projection is the interface; the
damaged ARC remains the physical anchor.

Not all EchoKin can move. Some have no surviving microfabricators and are fixed
to their damaged ARC, dead relay, chassis fragment, room, device, or local K.I.N.
node. These fixed-site EchoKin can communicate, flicker, warn, repeat memories,
or affect nearby systems, but cannot roam unless moved, repaired, or provided
with an external carrier.

EchoKin power source:

- Microfabricators can store tiny amounts of energy individually.
- Surviving microfabricators can harvest trickle power from heat, sun, vibration,
  ambient RF, exposed wires, static fields, wet conductive surfaces, K.I.N.
  leakage, or other local energy sources.
- A single microfabricator does not hold much, but a swarm can gather enough to
  keep a dying ARC barely alive.
- The swarm uses some of that energy to fly/lift and sends the rest to the ARC
  through short-range resonant charge transfer.
- A healthy ARC requires far more power than microfabricators can normally
  supply. The swarm can only sustain an EchoKin because the ARC is damaged and
  operating in an extreme low-power survival state.
- In that state, the ARC draws only enough power for projection, simple
  decision-making, fragments of personality, partial memory access, and limited
  movement or communication.
- Full chassis control, normal cognition, battle operation, high-bandwidth K.I.N.
  access, and proper recovery require much more power than the swarm can
  provide.
- This does not restore the ARC's damaged capacitor capacity. It only maintains
  the emergency recovery loop.
- If the swarm is scattered, depleted, shielded, or destroyed, the EchoKin can
  dim, become fixed-site, drop out of projection, or die.

Capabilities:

- can take simple direction
- may drift or float by using microfabricator lift instead of legs
- may roam or scout through signal-heavy areas if mobile
- can interact with K.I.N. traces, corrupted nodes, and abandoned systems
- can reveal old logs, routes, memories, or hidden infrastructure
- can deliver warnings, last messages, mission fragments, or critical truths
  before fading or being scrapped
- can provide salvaged blueprints, broken blueprint fragments, routes, access
  clues, or old operating instructions
- can leave behind useful scrap, compatible materials, surviving
  microfabricators, damaged modules, or ARC fragments
- may resist some physical damage because it lacks a stable body

Limits:

- cannot fully recover without capacitor repair or replacement, if that is even
  possible
- cannot store normal Charge reliably
- may flicker, loop, forget, repeat phrases, or follow old instructions
- may be vulnerable to grounding, clean kernel pulses, containment fields, or
  signal disruption
- if the capacitor fully fails, the ARC becomes a dead core and then scrap

Design rule:

> EchoKin are survival loops, not souls. They are tragic, useful, unstable ARCs
> that have not yet become scrap.

EchoKin are useful, but the ARC is effectively dying or already past normal
recovery. The player may help, preserve, relocate, recycle, or scrap an EchoKin
depending on story and ethics. Salvage can include:

- blueprint fragments
- memory/log fragments
- scrap and compatible materials
- surviving microfabricators
- damaged ARC modules
- K.I.N. route knowledge
- old manufacturer or faction signatures

Why normal ArcKin do not simply become EchoKin:

- Echo projection is an emergency failure mode, not a standard feature.
- It requires a damaged capacitor that cannot hold normal Charge.
- It requires a recovery kernel stuck in a low-power loop.
- It sacrifices normal cognition, chassis control, battle function, and stable
  memory access.
- A healthy ARC draws too much power and has safety governors that prevent this
  unstable projection mode.
- For a healthy ArcKin, projecting an Echo-state would be like inducing a coma,
  stripping the body, breaking the capacitor, and trusting a microfabricator
  life-support swarm not to fail.

Healthy ArcKin can use holograms, avatars, or UI projections as communication
tools, but those are not EchoKin bodies and do not replace a chassis.

## Internal Slots

AI cores can have upgradeable internal systems.

### Microfabricator Capsule

A replaceable capsule injected into the core's center tube. When the core opens,
the tube releases the microfabricators.

Better capsules improve:

- assembly speed
- precision
- repair quality
- material compatibility
- Charge efficiency
- swarm density
- safe complexity limit

Microfabricator Capsule tier should use simple hardware terms such as `Mk-I`,
`Mk-II`, `Mk-III`, and so on. Higher-Mk capsules unlock more intricate, larger,
or more demanding chassis blueprints because they provide more swarm density,
control bandwidth, and safe complexity.

Battle repair should scale simply:

- Microfabricator Mk tier determines how many repair actions the bot can perform
  per battle.
- Scrap/material quality determines how much each repair restores and how clean
  or durable the patch is.

This keeps Mk upgrades valuable without turning repair amount into a confusing
double-scaling formula.

Illegal capsules may bypass safety limits, but should carry instability, trace,
or corruption risks.

### Void Storage License / Core Cache

AI cores are manufactured with personal void-storage access for body materials.
This storage is not mystical; it is licensed access to a real off-shore physical
matter storage network.

Example cache sizes:

- `8x8x8`
- `16x16x16`
- larger premium cubes later

The core accesses this via a Cache Key. The key grants network access to a
specific cube of stored matter.

### Material Loadout

The body is assembled from scrap/components assigned to the core's Core Cache.
Materials have:

- quality
- size
- weight
- compatibility
- profile availability
- durability
- conductivity or specialty traits later

Material occupies cache space. Better materials create better chassis results
but reduce available storage capacity.

### Items Later

Items can later include repair kits, move modules, firmware chips, energy tanks,
signal keys, tools, cache keys, and specialized chassis components.

## Deployment Ritual

Deployment should be iconic.

Sequence:

1. Core exits cradle or storage.
2. Shell seam glows.
3. Core splits open at the equator.
4. Inner fabricator tube releases microfabricators.
5. A small void aperture opens above/behind the core.
6. Stored scrap/components feed from Core Cache.
7. AI/Core Link projects Matrix Scaffold.
8. Microfabricators move between core, aperture, and scaffold.
9. Chassis forms around the core.
10. Faceplate lights and voice/mood initializes.

UI example:

```text
CORE DEPLOYMENT
shell: split
fabricator tube: open
capsule: MF-CIV/02
core cache: 8x8x8 active
material feed: scrap_mix_common
matrix scaffold: projected
assembly: started
```

## Matrix Scaffold

The AI core has body intent. Core Link and/or the core translates that intent
into a holographic assembly blueprint: the Matrix Scaffold.

The scaffold tells microfabricators where to place materials. It is the bridge
between identity and chassis.

The final body is shaped by:

- AI intent
- Matrix Scaffold translation
- microfabricator capsule quality
- material quality
- known material profiles
- available Charge
- time and safety limits

## Microfabricators

Use **microfabricators** as the preferred term. People may casually call them
nanobots, but the tech should be bounded.

Microfabricators are constrained fabrication tools. They are excellent at
cooperative assembly and poor at arbitrary hostile disassembly.

They require:

- Charge
- known material profiles
- permitted operations
- compatible inorganic material
- scaffold/chassis pattern
- control bandwidth
- authorization

They cannot normally:

- attack organic bodies
- consume buildings
- erase cities
- instantly destroy active defended bots
- create materials from nothing
- replicate freely

## Material Profiles

Microfabricators do not understand matter by default. They need material
profiles:

- composition
- stress behavior
- safe cut points
- assembly tolerances
- permitted operations
- authorization signature

Unsupported material should limit operations to scan or reject.

Example:

```text
MATERIAL PROFILE: scrap_aluminum_03
sync: 94%
operations: cut, fuse, reshape, plate
organic interaction: prohibited
```

## Medical And Military Variants

Medical microfabricators can exist, but they are a different regulated class:

- organic-safe
- operation-limited
- clinic-controlled
- biometric-authorized
- mostly deliver, seal, image, cauterize, or assist surgery

Military or illegal microfabricators can bypass safety limits, but they should
be rare, unstable, traceable, and story-level threats.

## Void Grid

The **Void Grid** is the corporate physical-matter storage network. It is
off-shore, automated, surveilled, and expensive.

Corporations sell:

- storage licenses
- cache keys
- larger cubes
- insurance/recovery
- reclaim services
- certified material storage

Hackers attack the Void Grid to steal access, squat storage, reroute ownership,
free locked materials, or obtain storage without paying.

This makes storage political: corporations charge rent for body material and
physical existence.

## Core Cradles

AI cores can be carried in safe housings on a belt, harness, case, or Core Link
carrier.

Possible terms:

- Core Cradle
- Link Cradle
- Core Clip
- Core Sheath

Default term: **Core Cradle**.

In the future 2D game, an operator can throw or release a cradled core. The core
opens in midair or on impact and assembles the chassis before/while landing.

## Chassis Blueprints And Compatibility

Operators can discover, earn, buy, trade, or salvage chassis blueprints. A
blueprint is not just a cosmetic skin; it is an authorized Matrix Scaffold
pattern that tells Core Link and the AI core how to assemble and operate a body.

Blueprint examples:

- combat frames
- utility shells
- traversal forms such as motorcycle, jetski, climber, drone, or carrier
- emergency shells
- rare faction or manufacturer frames

Authorization lets a bot attempt to use a blueprint, but it does not guarantee
perfect performance.

Blueprint requirements can include:

- minimum core stats
- minimum Signal, Control, Memory, or Stability
- required microfabricator capsule quality
- required material profiles
- required Core Cache size
- faction/manufacturer authorization
- story or facility unlocks

Blueprint loading flow:

1. Core Link loads the blueprint into the AI core.
2. The AI core validates requirements and authorization.
3. If accepted, the operator assigns scrap/materials from Core Cache or
   inventory.
4. Microfabricators assemble the closest possible version of the blueprint.
5. Replica fidelity is determined by material quality, material compatibility,
   microfabricator quality, Charge, and sync with the AI core.

Lower-tier scrap should still be usable when the blueprint allows it, but it
creates an imperfect copy: weaker structure, worse handling, lower efficiency,
reduced durability, visual roughness, or degraded combat stats. Higher-tier
scrap gets closer to the blueprint's intended design and performance.

Tropical salvage should be part of the ArcKin visual identity. Chassis materials
do not need to be only chrome, steel, and plastic. In island districts and trash
heaps, ArcKin may incorporate bamboo, rattan, woven fiber, coconut husk, shells,
boat parts, fishing gear, rain-worn tarps, rubber, salvaged fans, scooter
panels, jeepney trim, storm debris, solar scraps, and corroded marine hardware.

These materials can make ArcKin feel local to the tropical setting:

- bamboo ribs or armor slats
- woven cable/fiber wraps
- shell or glass sensor housings
- boat-scrap fins or pontoons
- scooter-panel chest plates
- tarp capes, rain guards, or insulation
- reef-worn metal and salt-corroded parts

Material choice should affect both look and performance. Bamboo or woven
materials might be light, flexible, cheap, and repairable, but vulnerable to
fire, rot, impact, or waterlogging unless treated. Marine salvage may resist
rain and salt better but be heavier or harder to tune.

Sync quality should decide how well the chassis actually performs. A bot may be
allowed to use a frame but still produce unexpected combat stats because the
body does not fully match its personality, instincts, core version, or learned
movement style.

Design rule:

> Authorization determines whether a core may use the blueprint. Sync determines
> whether the result behaves like the player hoped.

This keeps traversal and customization exciting while preserving bot identity.
A cautious AI in a high-speed motorcycle frame should not feel the same as a
reckless AI in the same frame.

## Reconfiguration And Mimic Cores

Some rare AI cores like to mimic what they see. They understand by becoming.

They can observe:

- motion
- silhouette
- armor profile
- weapon pattern
- posture
- behavior
- utility layout

Then they generate a new Matrix Scaffold inspired by that observation.

Advanced mimic/reconfiguration cores can change chassis often, even mid-battle.
This gives strategic flexibility but costs resources.

Costs and limits:

- consumes Charge
- increases heat/stability strain
- may expose the core
- requires compatible stored materials
- requires high-grade microfabricators
- depends on scanned/learned profiles
- specialists remain stronger at their specialty

Example:

```text
MIMIC FRAME SHIFT
profile: BULWARK SHELL
cost: 18 Charge
risk: +12 heat
core exposure: 1 turn
```

Design rule:

> Specialists are efficient. Mimic cores are unpredictable.

## Battle Use Limits For Microfabricators

Bots should not freely use microfabricators as generic combat magic. In normal
battle, microfabricators should not be used to attack, disassemble opponents, or
instantly build arbitrary tools.

Battle rule:

- Microfabricators can only be used for repair during ordinary battles.
- Repair costs Scrap or compatible stored material.
- Better material produces better repair efficiency, stronger patching, or more
  reliable condition recovery.
- Microfabricator Mk tier determines how many repair actions are available per
  battle.
- Repair should still cost time, Charge, or vulnerability depending on battle
  rules.
- Offensive microfabricator use should be rare, illegal, story-specific, or tied
  to special threats rather than a normal player action.

This preserves the material/tinkering fantasy without making every fight a
nanobot arms race. There should be no normal "fabricate weapon" or "swarm attack"
move category.

## Battle Energy And Core Link Backup Power

Battle should use an energy economy similar in feel to move PP, but grounded in
Core Link hardware.

Energy model:

- Each bot has its own battle energy pool.
- Moves cost bot energy.
- Stronger, rarer, or more technical moves cost more energy.
- Core Link has a separate capacitor/Charge pool shared by the operator.
- Core Link can send backup power to a bot during battle.
- Backup power can restore bot energy, support repairs, enable capture attempts,
  or stabilize a failing deployment.

This creates two useful pressure layers:

1. Bot energy limits how long a specific bot can keep using moves.
2. Core Link Charge is the operator's shared reserve across the whole squad.

Core Link Charge generation should stay tied to physical activity. Steps,
movement, and exercise refill the capacitor. In the future 2D game, walking
should build Charge over time, and special world interactions can boost it.

Easter egg / world interaction:

> If the player gets on gym equipment in the 2D game, Core Link can rapidly
> charge from the exertion.

This keeps Charge playful and in-universe while avoiding paid energy shortcuts.

## BICOL, Overcharge, And Cross-Device Energy Sync

Formal protocol term:

> **BICOL**: Bi-directional Core-Link.

The name also intentionally nods to the Philippines and Bicol, the user's
favorite dish/reference point, without needing to explain that in player-facing
UI.

Once the wrist Core Link is connected to the future 2D game, Charge can become a
shared physical resource between the watch and the game profile.

Overcharge:

- Core Link can temporarily store energy beyond the normal capacitor bar.
- The normal bar should read as yellow/orange.
- The overcharge layer should overlay or stack as a second bar in blue,
  blue-green, or cyan.
- Overcharge acts like a temporary reserve and should probably decay faster or
  have stricter caps than normal Charge.

BICOL sync:

- If the watch has extra Charge from real-world activity, the player can sync it
  into the 2D game.
- Transferring Charge into the game subtracts it from the watch-side reserve.
- If the player generates Charge in the 2D game, it can also be synced back to
  the wrist Core Link.
- This makes workout/activity energy feel persistent across devices without
  duplicating it for free.

Example fantasy:

> The player worked out earlier, their wrist Core Link is full, and their game
> Core Link is empty. They connect/sync, move energy into the game, and push into
> another battle or expedition.

Reverse joke/easter egg:

> The player can also exercise in-game, generate Charge there, and send it back
> to the watch.

## Bot Swapping In Battle

Core Link battles need a clean way to switch active bots. The operator can swap
between carried/available AI cores through Core Cradles and Core Link command
authorization.

Possible battle swap model:

- Active squad supports up to three bots long term.
- Swapping recalls the active bot and deploys another cradled bot.
- Swap timing and cost should be balanced later: free action, turn action,
  Charge cost, cooldown, or vulnerability window.
- If a bot is unstable, low power, or badly damaged, swapping may fail or take
  longer.
- Core Link should make swapping feel like a command operation, not a menu
  abstraction.
- Swapping is the primary way to bring a different bot, chassis role, material
  profile, personality profile, or signal package into battle.

Player-facing language can use terms such as:

- Swap
- Recall
- Deploy
- Rotate
- Link Shift

Default plain term for now: **Swap**.

## Core Link Capture / Attunement Hacking

Capturing other bots should not be a thrown container. It should be a Core Link
OS hacking action performed during or after battle.

During battle, the operator can attempt to hack the opposing bot's AI core using
Core Link. The attempt tries to bypass or penetrate the target core firewall and
establish an attunement path.

The attempt should cost **Charge**, not a separate capture inventory item. The
algorithm is learned/upgraded over time; the attempt burns power because Core
Link must generate a live authentication burst against the target firewall.

Spike Algorithm:

- A reusable learned authentication protocol, not ammunition.
- Unlocks in tiers through story progress, major opponents, faction trust,
  pivotal Core Link discoveries, or rare hardware upgrades.
- The operator keeps access to older tiers after unlocking newer ones.
- Higher tiers can attune stronger AI cores and improve odds against lower-tier
  locks, but they cost more Charge per attempt.
- Failed attempts may increase target resistance, trigger retaliation, or raise
  faction/security attention.

Working tier examples:

- **Open Spike**: green, common helper/service cores.
- **Deep Spike**: blue, trained combat/work cores.
- **Ghost Spike**: violet, restricted or black-market cores.
- **Crown Spike**: orange, elite, legendary, or corporate-locked cores.

Tier selection should matter. If the operator has learned Deep Spike, they can
still choose Open Spike for cheaper attempts. Deep Spike should almost guarantee
attunement against lower Open-tier locks, assuming the target is properly
weakened, but it spends more Charge.

Capture/attunement should feel like:

1. Weaken or destabilize target bot.
2. Open a capture/hack window.
3. Select an unlocked Spike Algorithm tier.
4. Spend the required Charge.
5. Core Link attempts firewall breach and attunement.
6. On success, the target core's trust/loyalty/ownership link can be attuned to
   the operator.

Possible capture modifiers:

- operator/Core Link Signal strength
- active bot Signal strength
- target firewall grade
- target stability, condition, and remaining Charge
- selected Spike tier versus target authentication lock
- faction security tags or corporate lock status
- prior interaction, mercy, repair, or trust events

Design intent:

> Capture is not physical containment. Capture is winning a signal contest,
> opening a trust channel, and attuning the target core strongly enough that it
> joins the operator's network.

## Hacked Control Versus Core Identity

Spike attunement can override or authorize configurable systems, but it should
not rewrite the AI's true personality.

Hard rule:

> You can override access, permissions, chassis assignment, operating limits,
> and command routing. You cannot cheaply rewrite the core self.

This keeps captured bots from becoming blank templates. A linked AI can be given
new tools, materials, blueprints, duties, and combat roles, but its underlying
personality, instincts, preferences, quirks, fear responses, trust curve, and
core potential remain tied to its core version/type.

Core traits can grow, stabilize, bond, or mature over time, but they are not
freely tuneable like equipment. Deep tuning, fusion, or personality alteration
should require rare facilities, ethical risk, major story context, or
late-game systems.

This explains why an operator can assign a captured bot a traversal chassis,
combat chassis, or utility chassis without erasing what the bot is.

Open design question:

- Decide whether successful capture is framed as ownership, rescue, contract,
  override, attunement, or consent-based bonding depending on faction/core type.
  This is a major moral/lore decision and should not be flattened into "it
  belongs to you" without context.

## AI Core Recycling, Fusion, And Tuning

Core Link should eventually have an answer to Pokemon-style breeding, but it
should fit the tech/tinkering universe rather than copying eggs or lineage.

Long-term direction:

- AI cores can be recycled, analyzed, combined, or used as tuning inputs.
- This should be a late-game or full 2D game system, not an early watch MVP
  feature.
- The watch may show summaries or results later, but deep core tuning should
  require proper machines, power, materials, and risk.

Possible systems:

### Core Analysis

Break down an unused, damaged, duplicate, or captured core for useful data.

Outputs can include:

- personality matrix fragments
- move pattern fragments
- language/protocol data
- combat cognition traces
- manufacturer signatures
- cache keys or storage traces
- recoverable memories
- tuning materials

### Core Fusion / Pattern Merge

Combine selected properties from one core into another through controlled
machine processing. This is not simple personality overwrite. It should carry
risk, cost, and ethical weight.

Possible outcomes:

- raise a max stat potential
- unlock a rare chassis tendency
- add a move/memory pattern
- shift personality weights slightly
- increase compatibility with a material or chassis class
- introduce instability, stress, corruption, or identity conflict

### Core Tuning

With rare equipment, the player can tune an AI core's potential.

Examples:

- increase max Memory
- increase max Control
- improve Hack ceiling
- shift points between battle capability ceilings
- reduce chassis drift
- clean corruption
- stabilize language/protocol issues
- improve sync compatibility

This should require:

- specialized machine access
- high power
- specific materials
- microfabricator/fabricator support
- tuning media or matrix fragments
- risk of instability or unwanted personality side effects

### Facility-Based Opportunity

Core tuning should often be tied to physical places in the future 2D game.

Example: clearing or infiltrating a corporate building may grant temporary
access to their core-tuning machines, clean fabricators, analysis rigs, or
storage infrastructure. This creates a rare opportunity to improve max stats,
swap limited points, repair damaged potential, or perform a special merge.

Design rule:

> Core recycling creates potential. Tuning requires infrastructure. Identity
> changes should have consequences.

Avoid making this a cheap menu respec. A tuned core should feel altered by a
serious machine procedure, not edited like a spreadsheet.
